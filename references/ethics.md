# Ethics and compliance playbook

Use this file to reach a defensible verdict on an ethics, conduct, or performance-presentation scenario: which standard applies, whether it is violated, and what the member should do. The basis is the 2014 CFA Program curriculum. Page numbers are the `<!-- page N -->` markers in the knowledge-base (KB) files; "p." without a file means L1 Reading 2 (Guidance for Standards I-VII). Paths are in section 7.

## 1. Ground rules and edition notes

- **Who is bound.** CFA Institute members and CFA candidates, as individuals. A firm is bound only if it adopts the Code and Standards, and then states: "[Party] claims compliance with the CFA Institute Code of Ethics and Standards of Professional Conduct. This claim has not been verified by CFA Institute." (L1 R1 p.27). A non-member employee is bound only through firm policy that incorporates the Code (L2 Super Selection, p.256).
- **Default assumption.** Handbook examples treat every individual as a member and charterholder unless stated otherwise (L1 R1 p.24).
- **Enforcement.** The Professional Conduct Program (PCP) runs under a Designated Officer. Inquiries start from self-disclosure on the annual Professional Conduct Statement, written complaints, media or other public sources, or exam proctor reports. Outcomes: close the inquiry, issue a cautionary letter, or propose a sanction; if the sanction is rejected, a Disciplinary Review Committee hearing panel decides. Sanctions: public censure, suspension of membership and of the right to use the designation, revocation of the charter; candidates can be suspended from the program (L1 R1 p.26-27).
- **Edition.** The 2014 readings reproduce the 10th-edition Handbook (2010). The 9th edition (2005) introduced the I(A)-VII(B) numbering and the 10th keeps it; pre-2005 material is numbered differently. The only change to a standard's text from the 9th to the 10th edition: III(A) dropped its third sentence ("must determine applicable fiduciary duty and must comply with such duty..."), and the principle still applies through I(A) (L1 R1 p.25).
- **Later editions.** The 11th edition (2014 Handbook, used in curricula from 2015) renamed VII(A) "Conduct as Participants in CFA Institute Programs" and reworded Code component 5 (section 2). Neither change alters a verdict. Later Handbooks are not in the KB; if a question is set under a newer edition, say which edition the answer relies on.
- **Mental-state tests.** I(C) "knowingly" means knew or should have known. I(A) applies when the member knows or should know the conduct may contribute to a violation. II(B) requires intent to mislead. IV(C) asks whether the supervisor made reasonable efforts with adequate procedures.

## 2. Code of Ethics

Members of CFA Institute and candidates must (L1 R1 p.32):

1. Act with integrity, competence, diligence, respect, and in an ethical manner with the public, clients, prospective clients, employers, employees, colleagues in the investment profession, and other participants in the global capital markets.
2. Place the integrity of the investment profession and the interests of clients above their own personal interests.
3. Use reasonable care and exercise independent professional judgment when conducting investment analysis, making investment recommendations, taking investment actions, and engaging in other professional activities.
4. Practice and encourage others to practice in a professional and ethical manner that will reflect credit on themselves and the profession.
5. Promote the integrity of and uphold the rules governing capital markets. (11th edition: "Promote the integrity and viability of the global capital markets for the ultimate benefit of society.")
6. Maintain and improve their professional competence and strive to maintain and improve the competence of other investment professionals.

The Code states principles. A verdict should cite a Standard and use the Code only as support or where no Standard fits.

## 3. Standards I-VII

Each sub-standard has four parts: what it requires, the violation test, recommended procedures (M = member, F = firm), and classic traps.

### I. Professionalism

**I(A) Knowledge of the Law** (p.37-44)
- **Requires:** understand and comply with all applicable laws, rules, and regulations, including the Code and Standards; where they conflict, follow the stricter; do not knowingly participate or assist in a violation, and dissociate from any violation.
- **Violation if:** the member follows a looser law where the Code is stricter (or the reverse); knowingly participates or assists; or stays associated with conduct they know, or have reasonable grounds to believe, is illegal or unethical. Inaction combined with continued association counts as participation.
- **Procedures:** M: stay informed, review firm procedures, keep current reference files, know the laws where products are distributed, consult compliance or counsel. Dissociate in steps: raise it with the person, escalate to a supervisor or compliance, then step away (remove your name from reports, ask for another assignment, decline or end the client), and resign in extreme cases; document each step. F: code of ethics, information on applicable law, written protocol for reporting violations.
- **Traps:** the stricter-rule test applies per action, not per country (Exhibit 1, p.40-41: if the governing law defers to a locality with no law, the Code applies). Reporting to regulators is not required unless law requires it; reporting to CFA Institute is strongly encouraged but not required; failing to report is less likely to be a violation than failing to dissociate (p.39). Counsel's view that the regulator could not prove wrongdoing is no defence (Ex.1). Using marketing material with inflated performance is assisting a violation, even if someone else computed the figures (Ex.3).

**I(B) Independence and Objectivity** (p.45-55)
- **Requires:** reasonable care and judgment to stay independent and objective; do not offer, solicit, or accept any gift, benefit, compensation, or consideration that could reasonably be expected to compromise your own or another's independence.
- **Violation if:** accepting more than token value from a party seeking influence (broker, issuer, manager being hired); shading or suppressing a rating under pressure from investment banking, the issuer, buy-side holders, or sales; issuer-paid research with pay linked to conclusions or not disclosed; taking a personal allocation of a hot IPO.
- **Procedures:** M: token gifts only (ordinary business entertainment is fine if not meant to influence or reward); pay your own commercial travel and hotel, and accept issuer-arranged travel only when no commercial option exists, kept modest. F: written independence policy; analysts not reporting to banking or sales; analyst pay not linked to banking deals; if the firm will not allow negative opinions on a company, a restricted list with factual-only publication; a gift value limit (per gift or annual); pre-approval of IPO purchases and strict limits on private placements; a senior compliance officer.
- **Traps:** client gifts differ from third-party gifts. A client gift is acceptable if disclosed to the employer, before acceptance when possible and otherwise afterwards (p.46; Ex.8, Wimbledon tickets for good performance). An undisclosed broker's World Cup package given for directed commissions is a violation (Ex.7); a gift from someone seeking influence that could compromise judgment must be refused, and disclosure does not cure it.
- **More traps:** issuer-paid research is permissible with a flat fee negotiated in advance, independent of the conclusions, no warrants or equity, and full disclosure of the relationship and pay (p.49; I(C) Ex.2). Credit agency ratings may need independent validation (p.49). A buy-side manager who threatens a sell-side analyst over a downgrade compromises "another's" independence (p.47).

**I(C) Misrepresentation** (p.55-63)
- **Requires:** no knowing misrepresentation (an untrue statement, an omission, or a false impression) about analysis, recommendations, actions, qualifications, services, performance, or an investment's characteristics; no plagiarism.
- **Violation if:** guaranteeing a return on a volatile investment; using others' work, quotes, charts, or forecasts without attribution, or citing "leading analysts" without naming them; overstating personal credentials or firm capabilities; presenting an external manager's process as your own; continuing to use material you know or should know is false (Ex.4: a degree claimed in a brochure but never earned).
- **Procedures:** M: a summary of your own qualifications and services; keep copies of sources; attribute quotations, paraphrases, and summaries; verify third-party marketing material; keep webpages current. F: a written list of services and qualifications, designated spokespeople, review of correspondence, a plagiarism policy.
- **Traps:** data from "recognized financial and statistical reporting services or similar sources" needs no attribution (p.59). A guarantee built into a product's structure, or a loss an institution has agreed to cover, is not a misrepresentation. An unintentional error is not a violation, but once found, stop distribution and correct it (Ex.3). Work done for a firm belongs to the firm, which may keep using it without crediting departed analysts; an individual may not reissue an old report under their own name alone (p.58). Outsourced research may be redistributed if the member does not claim authorship. Leaving downside scenarios out of a model can misrepresent value (p.56).

**I(D) Misconduct** (p.64-66)
- **Requires:** no professional conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, or deceit; no act that reflects adversely on professional reputation, integrity, or competence.
- **Violation if:** lying, cheating, or stealing connected to professional life; drinking during business hours that impairs work; relying on someone else's due diligence instead of doing your own (a lack of effort can itself violate I(D), p.64).
- **Procedures:** F: a code of ethics, a published list of violations and sanctions, reference checks on new hires.
- **Traps:** personal bankruptcy alone is not misconduct; it is only if fraud was involved. Purely private behaviour is generally outside scope. Using PCP complaints to settle personal disputes is recognised as abuse of the process (p.64).

### II. Integrity of Capital Markets

**II(A) Material Nonpublic Information** (p.66-76)
- **Requires:** a member who has material nonpublic information (MNPI) that could affect the value of an investment must not act on it or cause others to act on it. This covers any instrument, including derivatives and funds.
- **Violation test:** material means disclosure would probably move the price, or reasonable investors would want it before deciding (weigh specificity, difference from public information, nature, source reliability, clarity of the price effect). Nonpublic means not yet disseminated to the market in general; a disclosure to a room of analysts or to the ten largest holders is still nonpublic (Ex.6). It is a violation when both hold and the member trades or causes others to trade, including by tipping family.
- **Procedures:** M: urge the issuer to disclose publicly; until it does, tell only designated compliance staff, do not trade or change recommendations, and never induce insiders to disclose. F, firewalls: compliance or legal controls communication between departments; watch, restricted, and rumor lists; documented procedures; restricted proprietary trading while the firm holds MNPI. F, separation: physical separation and no staff overlap; an analyst brought "over the wall" stays on the banking side until the information is public.
- **More procedures:** F: one officer decides materiality; employees report personal trades. Keep the watch list confidential, for monitoring; use a restricted list when the firm has or may have MNPI (a widely circulated restricted list can itself tip the market). Market makers go passive (take only the contra side of unsolicited orders) rather than withdraw; suspend risk arbitrage while a name is on the watch list; train staff.
- **Traps:** mosaic theory: conclusions from public plus nonmaterial nonpublic information may be acted on even if the conclusion would be material had an insider disclosed it (p.68-69; Ex.8-9); document the research under V(C). An unreliable tip, such as a doctor's takeover rumour, is not material (Ex.7). An analyst's own work is not MNPI to the analyst's clients, but advance word of a well-known analyst's rating change is MNPI for an outsider who learns it early (Ex.10). A pending large client order is MNPI for a broker or family members told about it (Ex.11-12). Assuming an executive "would not leak" is no defence, and losing money does not cure the violation (Ex.13).

**II(B) Market Manipulation** (p.76-80)
- **Requires:** no practices that distort prices or artificially inflate trading volume with intent to mislead market participants.
- **Violation test:** intent to mislead is decisive. Information-based manipulation: false rumors, pump-and-dump, a sensational report timed for effect. Transaction-based manipulation: trades that create a false impression of activity or price, or a dominant position used to move a related derivative or underlying.
- **Procedures:** the 2014 text lists none. Disclosure cures a volume-incentive program (Ex.5).
- **Traps:** legitimate strategies that exploit inefficiencies are allowed, as is a genuine large order in a thin market that moves the price. Selling and immediately repurchasing a stock for tax purposes is not manipulation (9th-ed. Handbook, II(B) Guidance, p.61; the 2014 text omits the example, but the case passes its intent test). Crossing trades between your own two funds to lift price and volume is manipulation, even if meant to help fund holders (Ex.4). An exchange that pays members for volume to show liquidity violates II(B) unless it discloses the arrangement (Ex.5). Manipulating model inputs to win a rating is manipulation (Ex.8). A speculative report timed to an issuer's quiet period violates II(B) and V(A) (Ex.3).

### III. Duties to Clients

**III(A) Loyalty, Prudence, and Care** (p.80-87)
- **Requires:** loyalty to clients; reasonable care and prudent judgment (the care a prudent person acting in a like capacity would use); act for clients' benefit; put client interests before the employer's and your own. Any legal fiduciary duty also applies, through I(A).
- **Violation if:** using client assets or brokerage for the member's or firm's benefit (churning to meet commission targets, Ex.6; paying higher commissions for research that does not benefit the client); buying sponsor stock for a pension plan to defeat a takeover and win business (Ex.1); disadvantaging a family member's fee-paying account to avoid an appearance of favouritism (Ex.7); ignoring governing documents or client guidelines.
- **Procedures:** M: identify the client; follow the investment policy statement (IPS) and governing documents; diversify unless guidelines say otherwise; judge each holding in total-portfolio context; with custody, send at least quarterly itemized statements and keep client assets segregated; when in doubt, disclose in writing and get client approval. F: written policies on objectives, suitability, diversification, regular reviews, fair allocation, disclosure of conflicts and compensation, proxy voting, confidentiality, and best execution.
- **Traps:** who is the client? For a pension plan or trust, the beneficiaries, not the sponsor or trustee who hired the manager. For a mandated fund, the duty is to manage to the mandate; whether the fund suits an individual investor is that investor's adviser's duty (Ex.9). Custody (any direct or indirect access to client funds) raises the duty.
- **Soft dollars, directed brokerage, proxies:** client brokerage belongs to the client; use it only for research that benefits the client. Directed brokerage (the client tells the manager to use its brokerage to buy goods or services for the client) is not a loyalty breach, but the manager must still seek best price and execution and should tell the client that directed brokerage may not achieve best execution (p.83). Proxy voting is part of the duty: not voting, or voting with management on non-routine issues without analysis, may violate III(A); a cost-benefit analysis can justify not voting some proxies; disclose the voting policy.

**III(B) Fair Dealing** (p.88-94)
- **Requires:** deal fairly and objectively with all clients in analysis, recommendations, investment action, and other professional activities.
- **Violation if:** a recommendation change is disclosed early to favoured clients; hot issues or profitable block trades go to favoured accounts (performance-fee accounts, large clients, gift-givers, accounts the member benefits from); premium service is offered selectively or harms other clients.
- **Procedures:** limit who knows about a pending recommendation; shorten the gap between decision and dissemination (a flash report if needed); bar trading before dissemination; disseminate simultaneously; keep a list of client holdings. Written allocation procedures: time-stamp orders and process them first in, first out; give every account in a block the same price and commission; allocate partial fills pro rata on order size, subject to minimum lot sizes; allocate new issues by client, not by portfolio manager, using advance indications of interest. Disclose allocation procedures and service levels; review accounts systematically.
- **Traps:** "fairly" does not mean "equally"; clients need not be reached at the same moment or get identical service. Premium service for higher fees is allowed if it does not disadvantage others, is disclosed, and is offered to everyone (p.88). In an oversubscribed issue, prorate among all clients for whom it is suitable; the member forgoes personal and immediate-family purchases, but family fee-paying accounts managed like other clients' accounts are still included (p.89).
- **More traps:** client consent never validates a patently unfair allocation, and disclosure does not cure an inequitable method (p.89, p.92). A changed recommendation should reach clients known to have acted on the earlier advice; a client placing an order contrary to the new view must be told before the order is accepted (p.89).

**III(C) Suitability** (p.95-101)
- **Requires:** in an advisory relationship, (a) a reasonable inquiry into experience, risk and return objectives, and financial constraints before acting, reassessed regularly; (b) a check that the investment suits the client's situation and is consistent with the written objectives, mandates, and constraints; (c) a judgment in total-portfolio context. When managing to a mandate, strategy, or style, act only consistently with the portfolio's stated objectives and constraints.
- **Violation if:** the same security goes into conservative and aggressive accounts without an account-by-account check (L2 Preston Partners); a fund drifts from its mandate (L2 Changing Investment Objectives: a low-volatility short-term government fund buying interest-only and principal-only mortgage strips); leverage or derivatives exceed the client's tolerance.
- **Procedures:** a written IPS covering client identification, return objectives and risk tolerance, constraints (liquidity, cash flows, investable funds, horizon, tax, legal, unique needs, proxy-voting responsibility), and a benchmark. Review at least annually and before material changes; document any review the client prevents. A suitability-test policy covering diversification impact, risk against tolerance, and fit with the strategy.
- **Traps:** a risky asset can be suitable in portfolio context, or when the objectives contemplate speculation. For an unsolicited trade the member knows is unsuitable, refrain or get the client's affirmative statement that suitability is not a consideration (p.96). Suitability is judged only on the information the client gives. A manager of a mandated fund is not responsible for the fund's suitability for individual investors.

**III(D) Performance Presentation** (p.101-104)
- **Requires:** reasonable efforts to make performance information fair, accurate, and complete.
- **Violation if:** a representative account or selected period is shown instead of the full record; terminated accounts are dropped; a prior firm's record is shown as the new firm's without disclosure (Ex.3); simulated or back-tested results are not labelled (Ex.4); the presentation implies past returns will recur; an analyst inflates the accuracy record of their recommendations.
- **Procedures:** comply with GIPS, the best method. Otherwise: consider the audience's sophistication; show weighted composites of similar portfolios; include terminated accounts, noting when they left; disclose whether results are simulated, from a prior entity, or gross, net, or after tax; keep the data and records. A brief presentation must offer the detail on request and say it is limited.
- **Traps:** a prior-firm record may be shown if the presentation says where it was earned and what the member's role was. The standard applies to pooled funds and analysts' track records, not only separate accounts. A guaranteed future return is also an I(C) issue.

**III(E) Preservation of Confidentiality** (p.104-106)
- **Requires:** keep information about current, former, and prospective clients confidential unless (1) it concerns illegal activities by the client, (2) the law requires disclosure, or (3) the client permits it.
- **Violation if:** the member reveals information received within the confidential relationship, such as a client's planned charitable gift passed to a charity the member serves (Ex.2), or a client's internal plans given to a donor (Ex.1).
- **Procedures:** share only with authorised colleagues working for that client; follow the firm's rules for storing electronic information.
- **Traps:** the duty survives the end of the relationship. If the law requires confidentiality even about illegal activity, keep it. For suspected client illegality, tell a supervisor or compliance and get legal advice; there may be a duty to disclose and to resign the account (Ex.3). Giving client information to the CFA Institute PCP is not a violation.

### IV. Duties to Employers

**IV(A) Loyalty** (p.107-115)
- **Requires:** in matters related to employment, act for the employer's benefit; do not deprive it of your skills, divulge its confidential information, or otherwise harm it.
- **Violation if:** soliciting clients or prospects before leaving, even after hours or socially (L2 Glenarm); taking client lists, records, files, or models without written permission; misappropriating trade secrets or business opportunities; starting a competing independent practice without the employer's consent.
- **Procedures:** M: before independent practice, tell the employer the services, expected duration, and compensation, and wait for consent; on leaving, follow the employer's client-notification policy and take no records. F: competition, termination, incident-reporting (whistleblowing), and employee-classification policies.
- **Traps:** preparing to compete is allowed; competing before leaving is not. After departure, skills, experience, and knowledge of former clients' names are not confidential; contacting former clients using public information is allowed unless a noncompete forbids it. Firm records kept at home must be returned or erased. Whistleblowing that breaks firm policy is justified only to protect clients or the market, not for personal gain (p.109). Client interests come before the employer's. Independent contractors are governed by their contract; employee status depends on the degree of control. Employers are not bound by the Code, though giving them a copy is encouraged.

**IV(B) Additional Compensation Arrangements** (p.115-116)
- **Requires:** do not accept gifts, benefits, compensation, or consideration that competes with, or might reasonably create a conflict with, the employer's interest unless all parties involved give written consent.
- **Violation if:** a client offers a contingent reward (Ex.1: use of a Monaco condominium in any year the portfolio returns at least 15%) and the member accepts without the employer's written consent; non-cash benefits for an outside directorship, such as free family club membership, are not disclosed (Ex.2).
- **Procedures:** an immediate written report to the employer of the nature, approximate amount, and duration of the arrangement, confirmed by the party offering it; e-mail counts as written.
- **Traps:** a gift already received from a client needs disclosure to the employer under I(B); an arrangement for future or contingent pay for client work needs written consent under IV(B). Non-monetary benefits count as compensation.
- **Outside activities: test the conflict, not the activity** (VI(A) p.140; IV(A) p.108-110; IV(B)). What must be reported to the employer is what could interfere with unbiased advice or with the employer's interests. Outside board seats are usually reportable (p.140), above all at companies the firm invests in, covers or does business with, or when the seat is paid (IV(B) Ex.2); an unpaid seat at a private company in an unrelated industry creates little conflict. Managing money for others, even unpaid and even for family, competes with an investment employer's business and should be disclosed; doing it for fees is independent practice that needs the employer's consent (IV(A)). When a question asks which activity is *least likely* to require disclosure, choose the one with no plausible conflict.

**IV(C) Responsibilities of Supervisors** (p.117-123)
- **Requires:** reasonable efforts to detect and prevent violations of laws, rules, regulations, firm policies, and the Code and Standards by anyone under the member's supervision or authority, members or not.
- **Violation if:** there are no procedures or they are inadequate; the supervisor knows or should know the procedures are not being followed; after a possible violation, the supervisor relies on the employee's assurances, or only reports upward and warns.
- **Procedures:** adequate procedures are a written, accessible, plain-language manual tailored to the firm, with a compliance officer who has authority and resources, a supervisory hierarchy, checks and balances, stated scope, documented monitoring and testing, permissible conduct, and reporting and sanctions. Once in place: disseminate, update, train, remind, include conduct in performance reviews, review employees' actions, enforce. On a violation: respond promptly, investigate thoroughly, restrict or increase supervision pending the outcome. Keep the code of ethics separate from detailed procedures.
- **Traps:** delegating supervision does not remove responsibility. A supervisor with reasonable, enforced procedures is not liable just because a violation got through. If the compliance system is inadequate, decline supervisory responsibility in writing until it is fixed (p.118). A compliance officer is a supervisor only with control or influence over the employee, such as power to hire, fire, reward, or punish (L2 Super Selection). Enforce non-investment policies, such as mandatory vacations, equally.

### V. Investment Analysis, Recommendations, and Actions

**V(A) Diligence and Reasonable Basis** (p.123-131)
- **Requires:** diligence, independence, and thoroughness; a reasonable and adequate basis, supported by appropriate research, for every analysis, recommendation, or action.
- **Violation if:** acting without adequate research; relying on secondary research (from inside the firm) or third-party research without checking that it is sound (assumptions, rigor, timeliness, objectivity); using quantitative models without understanding their inputs and limits or testing negative scenarios; hiring a sub-adviser without review.
- **Procedures:** F: a policy that research, ratings, and recommendations need a substantiated basis, approved by a supervisory analyst or review committee; written due-diligence guidance; measurable research-quality criteria; minimum scenario testing for models; criteria for data providers and for external advisers (their code of ethics, compliance and controls, quality of reported returns, adherence to strategy).
- **Traps:** a diligent recommendation that later loses money is not a violation (Ex.12). A member may rely in good faith on research the firm has vetted unless there is reason to doubt it. A member who disagrees with a group consensus that has a reasonable basis need not remove their name from the report (p.126). How much diligence is needed depends on role, product, and resources.

**V(B) Communication with Clients and Prospective Clients** (p.132-137)
- **Requires:** (1) disclose the basic format and general principles of the investment process, and promptly disclose material changes; (2) use reasonable judgment to identify the important factors and include them; (3) distinguish fact from opinion.
- **Violation if:** forecasts are presented as facts (Ex.2); clients are not told of a process or mandate change, such as a new quantitative overlay or the use of sub-advisers (Ex.4-8); principal risks, model limitations, or a security's basic characteristics are omitted.
- **Procedures:** keep the research behind each report so omitted factors can be supplied on request; with capsule recommendations, tell clients more analysis is available.
- **Traps:** any medium counts, including a one-word "buy". A narrow report is fine if it states its limited scope. Communicate upside and downside as expected total returns.

**V(C) Record Retention** (p.137-139)
- **Requires:** develop and keep records that support analyses, recommendations, actions, and investment-related client communications.
- **Violation if:** there are no records to support a recommendation, or a departing member takes records to a new firm.
- **Procedures:** archive meeting notes, company materials, model inputs and outputs, risk analyses, IPS meeting notes, adviser-selection criteria, and outside research; the firm usually carries the retention duty.
- **Traps:** records are firm property; a departing member must re-create the support at the new firm from public or company sources, not from memory, before reusing old research. With no regulatory guidance, CFA Institute recommends keeping records at least 7 years (p.138). Meeting local rules may satisfy V(C), but check that it does.

### VI. Conflicts of Interest

**VI(A) Disclosure of Conflicts** (p.139-146)
- **Requires:** full and fair disclosure of everything that could reasonably be expected to impair independence and objectivity or interfere with duties to clients, prospects, or the employer; disclosures must be prominent, in plain language, and effective.
- **Violation if:** any of these is undisclosed: ownership of recommended securities; directorships; investment banking, underwriting, or market-making relationships; special compensation (short-term bonuses, performance fees, fund fee rebates, agent options); close ties to company management.
- **Procedures:** disclose compensation that may conflict with client interests; footnote the amount and expiry of agent options in research; wall off directors from investment decisions; update disclosures when a conflict changes materially. If the firm forbids disclosure, document the request and consider dissociating.
- **Traps:** best practice is to avoid conflicts and disclose those that cannot be avoided; boilerplate fails the prominence test. A directorship creates three conflicts: duties to the company's shareholders versus clients, pay in company securities, and access to MNPI. Sell-side members disclose material beneficial ownership; buy-side members disclose their personal-trading reporting procedures. Beneficial ownership means a direct or indirect pecuniary interest, voting power, or the power to dispose of the shares.

**VI(B) Priority of Transactions** (p.146-151)
- **Requires:** transactions for clients and employers take priority over transactions in which the member is a beneficial owner. The order is clients, then employer, then personal.
- **Violation if:** front-running client orders or recommendations; taking scarce hot issues for a spouse's account ahead of clients (Ex.2); trading before clients have had an adequate chance to act; tipping accounts in which the member has a beneficial interest.
- **Procedures:** avoid IPOs and pre-clear any participation; strict limits on and pre-clearance of private placements; blackout or restricted periods around client trades for everyone in the investment decision process. Reporting: holdings at hire and at least annually, duplicate confirmations and statements, pre-clearance. Disclose personal-trading policies to investors on request, in substance rather than boilerplate.
- **Traps:** beneficial-ownership accounts include family accounts (spouse, children, other immediate family), trusts, and retirement accounts. A family account that is an ordinary fee-paying client account must be treated like any other client, neither favoured nor disadvantaged (Ex.3; III(A) Ex.7), though pre-clearance and reporting may still apply. A personal sale contrary to current advice (to pay tuition or meet a margin call) is allowed if clients are not disadvantaged, the member does not profit from client trades, and the rules are followed. Co-investing with clients is not in itself a conflict.

**VI(C) Referral Fees** (p.151-155)
- **Requires:** disclose to the employer, clients, and prospective clients, as appropriate, any compensation, consideration, or benefit received from or paid to others for recommending products or services.
- **Violation test:** a violation if the disclosure is not made before the client enters into the formal agreement, or if it omits the nature of the benefit (flat or percentage, one-time or continuing, performance-based, non-cash such as research) or its estimated value.
- **Procedures:** the firm may ban referral fees outright; otherwise it sets an approval procedure, notifies clients of approved programs, and receives at least quarterly reports on the amount and nature of the fees.
- **Traps:** the standard covers internal referral payments between departments of the same firm (Ex.2), and fees paid as well as received. Consideration includes cash, soft dollars, and in-kind benefits. The Code requires disclosure even where local law does not (the I(A) stricter-rule example, p.38).

### VII. Responsibilities as a CFA Institute Member or CFA Candidate

**VII(A) Conduct as Members and Candidates in the CFA Program** (p.155-160; 11th-ed. title "Conduct as Participants in CFA Institute Programs")
- **Requires:** no conduct that compromises the reputation or integrity of CFA Institute or the CFA designation, or the integrity, validity, or security of CFA exams.
- **Violation if:** cheating or helping someone cheat; breaking testing policies (calculator, personal belongings, Candidate Pledge); revealing exam questions, or the broad topics and formulas tested or not tested; a volunteer or grader disclosing questions, deliberations, or scoring (Ex.8); false statements on the Professional Conduct Statement or in continuing education; misusing an association with CFA Institute.
- **Traps:** criticising CFA Institute or the exam's difficulty is allowed if no exam content is disclosed. Studying curriculum material in groups is fine.

**VII(B) Reference to CFA Institute, the CFA Designation, and the CFA Program** (p.160-166)
- **Requires:** do not misrepresent or exaggerate the meaning or implications of membership, the designation, or candidacy.
- **Violation if:** implying superior performance or competence ("CFA charterholders achieve better results"); using "CFA" as a noun ("a CFA", "he is a Chartered Financial Analyst"); writing "C.F.A." or "cfa" or altered forms ("CFA-type", "Swiss-CFA"); using the mark in a firm name, or the CFA logo to identify a firm (the logo is for individuals only); claiming a partial designation ("CFA Level II", "CFA (Passed Finalist)") or an expected completion date; using the designation while membership has lapsed because the annual Professional Conduct Statement was not filed or dues were not paid (Ex.2-3).
- **Correct usage:** "Jane Smith, CFA"; "CFA charterholder"; "passed Level I of the CFA examination in 2010"; "Level III candidate in the CFA Program". "Passed all three levels in consecutive years" (or "on the first try") is a fact and may be stated, but not linked to superior ability (Ex.1). Opinions on the program's merits are fine if framed as opinion. After passing Level III but before the charter: "may be eligible for the charter on completion of the work-experience requirement".
- **Candidacy and status:** a candidate is enrolled for a specified exam or awaiting results. A lapsed member may say they held membership or the charter in the past; "retired" status requires formal reclassification. The order of CFA and other designations, such as a PhD, is personal choice.
- **Procedures:** brief the marketing, legal, and compliance departments on VII(B); use centrally approved templates.

## 4. Decision procedure for any scenario

1. **Actors and roles.** For each person: member, candidate, or neither (bound only through firm policy)? Analyst, portfolio manager, supervisor (do they have control or influence?), compliance officer, employee, or contractor? For each counterparty: client, prospective client, or former client; the employer; a beneficiary (pension or trust); a fund investor (owed management to the mandate only); an issuer or broker.
2. **Facts.** Build a timeline: who knew what and when; what was done or not done; what was disclosed, to whom, when, and in what form (written? before the engagement?); who consented.
3. **Map facts to standards** with the trigger table below. Expect several standards; III(A) and I(C) often accompany others.
4. **Apply each violation test** from section 3. Check the elements: knew or should have known; material and nonpublic; intent to mislead; reasonable efforts and adequate procedures; written consent; timing of disclosure; who the client is.
5. **Check the safe harbors:** mosaic theory; recognized statistical sources; legitimate or tax-motivated trading; a client gift disclosed to the employer; fair rather than equal treatment; directed brokerage; a group consensus with a reasonable basis; a diligent recommendation that lost money; reasonable procedures that still failed; preparing, not yet competing.
6. **Verdict.** For each actor, state "violation of X" or "no violation of X" and the fact that decides it. Separate the firm's conduct (not bound unless it adopted the Code) from the member's.
7. **Remedy.** What the member should have done and should do now: stop and correct; disclose; obtain written consent; dissociate (escalate, remove your name, decline the work, resign as a last resort); reallocate or make the client whole; document; consult counsel; for supervisors, fix the procedures. Reporting to regulators is required only if the law requires it; reporting to CFA Institute is encouraged.
8. **Sanity check** (L3 R3 p.207-208). Would the client agree if told all the circumstances? Would a full, accurate press account enhance or damage the member's and firm's reputation? Is this what a leader would do?

| Fact pattern | Test first |
|---|---|
| Gifts, trips, entertainment, perks from brokers, issuers, managers | I(B); IV(B) if contingent future compensation; VI(A) |
| Pressure on a rating or report from banking, issuer, sales, PMs | I(B), V(A), VI(A) |
| Copied material, credentials, guarantees, uncorrected errors | I(C) |
| Tips, overheard or selectively disclosed information, pending big orders | II(A), VI(B) |
| Trades or rumors that move price or volume | II(B) |
| Pension or trust assets, proxies, soft dollars, churning, custody | III(A) |
| Block or IPO allocation, recommendation changes, service tiers | III(B), VI(B) |
| Client profile, IPS, mandate drift, unsolicited orders | III(C), V(B) |
| Track records, composites, simulated or prior-firm results | III(D), I(C), GIPS |
| Client information leaving the relationship | III(E) |
| Leaving a firm, side business, taking files, whistleblowing | IV(A), V(C) |
| Supervisor learns of a problem, weak compliance system | IV(C), I(A) |

**Illustrative example (invented facts, not from the curriculum).** Lena Ortiz, CFA, a portfolio manager at an adviser, runs a corporate pension plan's equity account, which holds the sponsor's stock. The sponsor's CFO asks her to vote the plan's shares for management in a contested board election and hints the firm will win the sponsor's other mandates. The same week, a broker that executes much of her trading offers her a ski weekend. She votes with management without analysis, accepts the trip, and tells no one.
- Step 1, actors: Ortiz is a member and portfolio manager. The clients are the plan's participants and beneficiaries, not the CFO or sponsor. Her employer is the adviser. The broker is a third party seeking influence.
- Step 2, facts: she voted without analysis, motivated by new business for the firm; she accepted a benefit of more than token value from a broker whose business she controls; she told neither her employer nor her clients.
- Steps 3-4, map and test: the proxy vote falls under III(A). Proxies have economic value and a contested election is not routine, so voting blindly to win business puts the firm's interest above the beneficiaries': a violation. The trip falls under I(B), with VI(A) on disclosure. It could reasonably be expected to compromise her objectivity in choosing brokers: a violation (compare Ex.7).
- Step 5, safe harbors: a cost-benefit argument for not voting does not apply to a non-routine election; the client-gift exception does not apply because the broker is not a client.
- Step 6, verdict: violations of III(A) and I(B). Her supervisor should review the facts under IV(C).
- Step 7, remedy: Ortiz analyses the ballot and votes on the merits for the beneficiaries (correcting the vote if still possible), declines the trip or pays for it herself, and reports the offer and the CFO's approach to compliance. The firm adopts written proxy-voting and gift policies with a value limit and discloses the proxy policy to clients.
- Step 8: told everything, the plan participants would not approve, so the verdict holds.

## 5. GIPS essentials (2010 edition; L1 R3-R4, L3 R35)

- **Purpose.** Fair representation and full disclosure of performance, so firms can be compared. Voluntary, ethical, self-regulatory standards that target representative-account cherry-picking, survivorship bias, and selective time periods (L1 R3 p.185-186).
- **Edition and structure.** The 2010 edition is effective from 1 January 2011 and has nine sections: 0 Fundamentals of Compliance, 1 Input Data, 2 Calculation Methodology, 3 Composite Construction, 4 Disclosure, 5 Presentation and Reporting, 6 Real Estate, 7 Private Equity, 8 Wrap Fee/SMA. It also includes the Valuation Principles, Advertising Guidelines, and Verification procedures. Each provision is a requirement ("must") or a recommendation ("should") (L1 R3 p.188; L1 R4 p.192-193). A 2020 edition later replaced it; it is not in the KB, and answers here follow 2010.
- **Who can claim compliance.** Only investment management firms that actually manage assets. Not individuals; not plan sponsors or consultants unless they manage assets (they may endorse GIPS or require it of their managers); not software or vendors; never a single product or composite. Compliance is firm-wide.
- **No partial claims.** No "in compliance except for..." (0.A.6); no claim that a calculation method is "in accordance with" GIPS (0.A.7); no GIPS claim for a single portfolio's performance except when reporting to that client (0.A.8). Where law conflicts with GIPS, follow the law and disclose the conflict.
- **Definition of the firm.** An investment firm, subsidiary, or division held out to clients as a distinct business entity (0.A.12); the broadest definition, covering all offices under the same brand, is recommended (0.B.3). Total firm assets are all discretionary and non-discretionary assets, fee-paying or not (0.A.13). Organizational changes must not alter historical composite performance (0.A.15).
- **Composites (3.A.1-3.A.10, L1 R4 p.200-201).** Every actual, fee-paying, discretionary portfolio must be in at least one composite; non-discretionary portfolios are excluded; non-fee-paying ones may be included with disclosure. Composites are defined by mandate, objective, or strategy, with criteria set in advance and never applied retroactively: no cherry-picking, and no linking simulated or model results to actual ones.
- **Composite membership over time.** New portfolios join on a timely and consistent basis. Terminated portfolios stay in the history up to their last full measurement period. A portfolio switches composites only after a documented mandate change, and its history stays with the old composite. Carve-outs (from 2010) need their own cash. Minimum-asset and significant-cash-flow rules are set in advance, per composite.
- **Required history.** At least 5 years initially (or since inception), adding a year each year until there are at least 10. Non-compliant periods may be linked only before 1 January 2000, with the periods of non-compliance disclosed.
- **Calculation and input data.** Total returns; time-weighted returns adjusted for external cash flows, with sub-periods geometrically linked; valuation at least monthly (from 2001), on large cash flows (from 2010), and at fair value (from 2011); trade-date accounting (from 2005); accrual accounting for fixed income; no annualizing of periods under a year.
- **Presentation items (5.A.1, summary).** For each annual period: composite returns labelled gross or net of fees; the benchmark's total return; number of portfolios (if more than five); composite assets; total firm assets or composite assets as a percentage of firm assets; internal dispersion (if more than five portfolios for the full year); and from 2011, the three-year annualized ex-post standard deviation of composite and benchmark.
- **Disclosures (4.A, summary).** Compliance statement; firm definition; composite and benchmark descriptions; fee basis and fee schedule; currency; dispersion measure; composite creation date; availability on request of the composite list and of valuation and calculation policies; material leverage, derivatives, and short positions; significant events; periods of non-compliance before 2000; any redefinition of the firm.
- **Compliance statement (4.A.1, L1 R4 p.201-202), used only in a compliant presentation.** Unverified: "[Firm] claims compliance with the Global Investment Performance Standards (GIPS®) and has prepared and presented this report in compliance with the GIPS standards. [Firm] has not been independently verified." Verified: the same first sentence, then "[Firm] has been independently verified for the periods [dates]. The verification report(s) is/are available upon request.", then the standard sentence on the scope of verification.
- **Prospective clients.** Make every reasonable effort to give every prospect a compliant presentation (one given in the previous 12 months suffices); supply the composite list and any listed composite's presentation on request; keep terminated composites on the list for 5 years.
- **Verification (L1 R4 p.222-226).** Optional but recommended (0.B.2), and done only by an independent third party, never by the firm. Always firm-wide: claiming a single composite is "verified" is prohibited. It tests (1) whether all composite construction requirements are met firm-wide and (2) whether the policies and procedures are designed to calculate and present performance in compliance. It does not ensure any specific composite presentation is accurate. The minimum period is one year. A performance examination is optional extra testing of a specific composite at a verified firm.
- **Link to the Standards.** GIPS compliance is the best way to satisfy III(D); a false claim of GIPS compliance also violates I(C).

## 6. Firm-level codes

**Asset Manager Code of Professional Conduct** (L3 R6)
- **Scope.** Firms ("Managers") running separate accounts or pooled funds, hedge funds included. Adopted by senior management or the board and applied to all employees; the firm-level counterpart of the individual Code and Standards.
- **Six general principles.** Managers must (1) act in a professional and ethical manner at all times; (2) act for the benefit of clients; (3) act with independence and objectivity; (4) act with skill, competence, and diligence; (5) communicate with clients in a timely and accurate manner; (6) uphold the applicable rules governing capital markets.
- **Six areas, A-C.** A. Loyalty to clients: client interests first; confidentiality; refuse gifts or relationships that affect independence. B. Investment process and actions: reasonable care; no manipulation; fair dealing; reasonable basis; follow the mandate and disclose style changes; suitability for separate accounts. C. Trading: no use of MNPI; client trades first; commissions pay only for products that directly assist investment decisions, not firm management; best execution; fair trade allocation.
- **Six areas, D-F.** D. Risk management, compliance, and support: policies and procedures; a compliance officer; accurate client information confirmed or reviewed by an independent third party; records; qualified staff and resources; a business-continuity plan; a firm-wide risk management process. E. Performance and valuation: fair, accurate, relevant, timely, and complete performance; fair-market prices, with good-faith fair value where no quote exists. F. Disclosures: ongoing, timely, truthful communication, covering conflicts, regulatory or disciplinary actions, the investment process (including lock-ups, leverage, derivatives), fees and costs, soft or bundled commissions and how they benefit clients, performance, valuation methods, shareholder voting policies, trade allocation policies, audit results, significant personnel changes, and risk management processes.
- **Claiming compliance.** Every provision or nothing: partial claims ("complies except for...", "complies with parts A, B, and C") are prohibited. Statement: "[Firm] claims compliance with the CFA Institute Asset Manager Code of Professional Conduct. This claim has not been verified by CFA Institute." The firm must notify CFA Institute, which does not verify claims. The appendix guidance is recommended, not required (L3 R6 p.273).

**Research Objectivity Standards** (L2 R4)
- **Scope.** Sell-side firms that sell research for cash or soft commissions: investment banks, broker-dealers, independent research firms. Adoption is voluntary and complements the individual Code. Each standard has requirements and recommended procedures.
- **Required, 1-6.** (1) A written research-objectivity policy given to clients, prospects, and staff, with supervisory procedures and a senior officer attesting annually. (2) In public appearances, disclose conflicts to the host and, where possible, the audience. (3) A reasonable and adequate basis, reviewed by a supervisory analyst other than the author or a review committee. (4) Analysts separated from investment banking: they do not report to it, and banking cannot review, change, approve, or reject research. (5) Analyst pay tied to research quality and accuracy, not directly to banking deals. (6) No sharing the rating or price target with the subject company before publication; no promising favourable coverage or threatening to change it.
- **Required, 7-11.** (7) Personal trading: no front-running by covered employees or their families; no trading against the latest recommendation except in extreme financial hardship; no pre-IPO purchases in covered companies or the analyst's industry. (8) Timely, regular research. (9) Compliance and enforcement with sanctions, monitoring, and audit records. (10) Full and fair disclosure of conflicts. (11) A rating system that is useful to investors and helps them judge suitability.
- **Recommended.** Quiet periods of 30 calendar days after an IPO and at least 10 after a secondary offering, with no analyst roadshows. Restricted trading periods of at least 30 calendar days before and 5 after a report. Disclose whether the firm holds 1% or more of the subject company's equity. Relative ratings name their benchmark (L2 R4 p.232-236).

**Soft Dollar Standards** (L2 R3)
- **Principles.** Brokerage is the client's property. The manager must seek best execution, minimize transaction costs, and use client brokerage to benefit clients; brokerage must not be allocated by the number of client referrals a broker provides. The standards cover proprietary (in-house) and third-party research, in agency and principal trades.
- **What counts as research.** Its primary use must directly assist the investment decision-making process, not the management of the firm. Three-level test (L2 R3 p.211-212): (1) define the product narrowly (a workstation running research software can count; the electricity to run it cannot); (2) its primary use must directly assist investment decisions (a Bloomberg terminal kept as an office convenience fails); (3) for mixed-use products, pay with client brokerage only the research share (50% research use means 50% soft dollars), documented and re-evaluated at least annually. If the manager cannot decide and document that a product is research, it must not pay for it with client brokerage.
- **Client relationships.** Required: disclose soft dollar arrangements before engaging in them for a client's account. Recommended: over time, all clients should benefit from the research. Using principal-trade brokerage for other accounts needs disclosure and prior consent; where ERISA or the Investment Company Act of 1940 applies, consent cannot waive the requirement that the generating account benefit.
- **Directed brokerage.** Not a breach in itself, because the brokerage is the client's asset. Required: never use other clients' brokerage to pay for a directed arrangement. Recommended: tell the client the duty of best execution continues and that committing a fixed share of brokerage can impair execution and research; get a fiduciary's written assurance that the arrangement benefits the client.
- **Disclosure.** Plain-language policies; the types of research received, the extent of use, and any affiliated broker; whether research benefits clients other than those whose trades generated it; and that more information is available on request (what the firm obtained, by broker; the client's soft commissions and directed brokerage).
- **Records (required).** Arrangements and broker agreements; research providers and products; the basis for mixed-use allocations and how each product assists investment decisions; evidence of compliance, naming the person responsible; client disclosures and authorizations.
- **Compliance statement.** Voluntary. A manager claiming compliance must give the client, at least annually, a statement that the soft dollar arrangements for that account comport with the CFA Institute Soft Dollar Standards.
